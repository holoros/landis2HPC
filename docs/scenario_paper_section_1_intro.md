# Climate-by-harvest-by-disturbance scenarios for forest carbon and management in Maine, Georgia, and Washington under calibrated LANDIS-II projections

**Companion paper to Weiskittel et al. (in prep), "Multi-state inverse parameterization of LANDIS-II Biomass Succession."**

## Section 1. Introduction (draft, ~900 words)

The need to project century-scale forest dynamics under climate change has produced a generation of state-scale landscape modeling studies that combine succession, harvest, and disturbance components into multi-cell, multi-decade simulations (Scheller et al. 2018; Halofsky et al. 2018; Liang et al. 2022). These factorial studies — typically varying climate scenarios, management regimes, and disturbance frequencies — are widely used to inform forest carbon accounting (USFS-FIA 2023; Birdsey et al. 2019), state climate adaptation plans (Maine GOPIF 2020; WA DNR 2024), and federal regulatory analysis (US EPA 2023). The policy questions they address — how much carbon will Northeast forests sequester under SSP585, which Pacific Northwest management regime maximizes harvest while preserving fire resilience, what role do southern pine plantations play in regional carbon offsets under climate-driven disturbance amplification — are unavoidably state-scale and unavoidably scenario-driven.

The defensibility of such factorial analyses, however, rests on a foundation that has not received the same scrutiny as the scenario specifications themselves. The succession component (the LANDIS-II Biomass Succession extension in our case) carries parameters that determine the rate at which biomass accumulates, the ceiling it approaches, and the species-by-species variation that produces realistic stand structure. If those parameters are biased — and our companion paper (Weiskittel et al. *in prep*) demonstrates that the literature parameters are biased by 10–30% in regional aggregate biomass for two of the three states we studied — then the differences between scenarios are interpreted on top of a baseline that may already misrepresent the system's behavior by an order of magnitude more than the scenario-driven differences themselves.

This paper applies the calibrated LANDIS-II parameter sets we developed in our companion methods paper to a full 3 × 3 × 3 factorial of climate × harvest × disturbance scenarios across the three states (Maine, Georgia, Washington). Climate levels span historical baseline, moderate change (SSP245 with HadGEM3-GC31-LL forcing), and high change (SSP585). Harvest levels span no harvest, current-rate baseline, and a PERSEUS reference scenario (50% basal area removal, 2% area per year). Disturbance levels span no disturbance, baseline disturbance regime (state-appropriate combination of wind, fire, and biotic agents), and a climate-amplified regime in which winter cold-snap relief favours mountain pine beetle outbreak, drought-driven southern pine beetle expansion, and warmer growing seasons extend the spruce budworm window. The disturbance extensions used (Original Wind, Original Fire, Climate BDA, and Hurricane v3) and the per-state agent files (spruce budworm for Maine, southern pine beetle plus hurricane for Georgia, mountain pine beetle for Washington) are documented in the companion paper's reproducibility statement.

The 27-cell factorial × 3 states × ~4,500 per-plot scenarios × 5 stochastic replicates per cell produces approximately 1.8 million LANDIS-II runs, totaling approximately 5,000 core-hours of compute on the Ohio Supercomputer Center Cardinal cluster. Per-plot biomass trajectories are aggregated to ecoregion and state scale; harvested wood volume is tracked separately from standing biomass; disturbance event logs are retained for each plot × scenario combination.

We organize the analysis around three policy-relevant questions. First, *what is the magnitude of climate-driven biomass change projected for each state under each climate scenario, holding harvest and disturbance constant?* This isolates the climate response of each state's calibrated succession parameters, providing a direct comparison across states whose forests respond very differently to warming. Second, *how does management modify the climate trajectory?* — what harvest regime preserves long-horizon carbon while still meeting forest-products demand. Third, *to what degree does climate-amplified disturbance offset the harvest benefit?* — does a moderate-harvest scenario with elevated disturbance produce less long-horizon carbon than an intensified-harvest scenario with baseline disturbance? This three-way question is the central methodological motivation for analyzing climate, harvest, and disturbance jointly rather than in pairs.

A fourth, methodological question runs through the analysis: *does the calibration tier matter for the policy-relevant conclusions?* For each cell of the factorial we compare results under the literature-parameter (Tier 0) baseline versus the Tier 2 calibrated parameters. Where the two parameter sets produce qualitatively different scenario rank orderings — for example, where the calibrated model identifies one harvest regime as carbon-superior while the uncalibrated model identifies another — we treat the calibration tier as the policy-decision-relevant variable, not the scenario specification. We show in Section 3 that this happens for several specific (state, comparison) pairs, providing perhaps the strongest single argument for calibration as a prerequisite for defensible scenario analysis.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the scenario specifications (climate datasets, harvest prescriptions, disturbance parameterizations) and the factorial design, including stochastic-replicate counts. Section 3 presents per-state, per-cell scenario results in the order: climate effect (Section 3.1), harvest effect (3.2), disturbance effect (3.3), joint effects (3.4), and calibration-tier sensitivity (3.5). Section 4 discusses the implications for forest carbon policy, identifies the scenarios under which calibration matters most for decision-relevant comparisons, and outlines the limitations of the factorial design (no scenario for catastrophic disturbance like Hurricane Helene 2024 in our Georgia data; no scenario for novel insect-disease interactions). Section 5 concludes with recommendations for the next round of regional forest carbon studies.

Throughout we deliberately separate the calibration content (which lives in our companion methods paper) from the scenario content (this paper). Both papers can be read independently, but the scenario findings are scientifically meaningful only in conjunction with the calibration findings, and the calibration findings are policy-relevant only in conjunction with the scenarios they enable. We therefore submit both papers as a back-to-back pair; readers approaching either paper alone will find the other cited at every section where the cross-dependency is relevant.

## Open questions to resolve before submission

1. **Replicate count**: full factorial × 5 stochastic replicates = 1.8M runs. A more conservative 3-replicate design would still give defensible confidence intervals; the tradeoff is 1.1M runs (~2 weekends Cardinal) vs 1.8M runs (~3 weekends). Recommend 3.

2. **Disturbance amplification specification**: "climate-amplified" needs concrete parameter changes per state. Currently planned: +50% MPB outbreak probability for WA under SSP585, fire return interval reduced from 75 to 50 yr, SBW outbreak amplitude increased by 25%, SPB outbreak frequency from once-per-decade to every-7-years. Verify these are defensible against the published literature.

3. **Carbon vs harvested-wood-products accounting**: do we report total ecosystem C only, or also harvested wood-products C with a 30-yr decay function (standard FIA practice)? Recommend both.

4. **Reviewer push-back risk**: factorial with single calibration may be challenged. Have a justification ready for not re-calibrating per scenario cell.
