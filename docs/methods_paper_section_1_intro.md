# Multi-state inverse parameterization of LANDIS-II Biomass Succession against the FIA inventory cycle: a calibration ladder for Maine, Georgia, and Washington forests

## Section 1. Introduction

**Draft, 2026-05-15. ~950 words. Target: Environmental Modelling & Software.**

Forest landscape models are increasingly the only tool available for projecting how regional carbon stocks, forest composition, and management tradeoffs will evolve over the coming century. Among these models, LANDIS-II (Scheller and Domingo 2007; Scheller et al. 2007) has become the most widely adopted in North America, with applications spanning carbon accounting (De Bruijn et al. 2014), climate-vulnerability assessment (Halofsky et al. 2018; Janowiak et al. 2018), wildfire-management planning (Sturtevant et al. 2009; Lucash et al. 2017), and insect-outbreak forecasting (Sturtevant et al. 2012). The Biomass Succession extension (Scheller and Mladenoff 2004), used in roughly two-thirds of recent LANDIS-II applications, simulates cohort-level aboveground biomass dynamics conditional on a small set of species-by-ecoregion parameters: aboveground net primary productivity ceiling (ANPPmax), maximum biomass ceiling (BiomassMax), establishment probability, and mortality probability. These four parameters control essentially all of the long-horizon biomass behavior that downstream applications then build on.

Despite this central role, ANPPmax and BiomassMax are rarely calibrated against landscape-scale empirical data. They are most often transferred from regional process-model outputs (e.g., PnET-II runs), from published growth-curve studies, or from simple expert-elicitation exercises (De Bruijn et al. 2014; Lucash et al. 2017; Liang et al. 2022). The growth-curve studies that inform these literature values were generally conducted on highly productive, vigorous plots — exactly the plots that yield clean growth curves. Forest landscapes, however, include the full productivity gradient: stagnated stands, partly defoliated stands, edaphically constrained stands, and stands with sub-clinical disturbance impacts that nevertheless suppress growth. There is a strong a-priori reason to expect literature ANPPmax to be systematically biased upward relative to the landscape mean, and a corresponding reason to expect uncalibrated LANDIS-II to over-project regional biomass — particularly in long horizons that compound the bias.

Existing efforts to address this gap have used a range of approaches: hand-tuning to match aggregate inventory totals (Liang et al. 2018), Bayesian calibration against eddy-covariance towers (Dietze et al. 2014), or independent re-derivation of species parameters from local growth studies (Foster et al. 2017). None of these directly use what is, for the United States, the most spatially extensive and methodologically consistent forest measurement dataset available: the USDA Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) annualized plot network (Bechtold and Patterson 2005). The FIA design provides each state with a sample of fixed permanent plots remeasured on a five-to-ten-year cycle, with consistent tree-level diameter and species protocols across cycles. For an untreated subset of plots — those not subject to harvest, fire, or salvage during the remeasurement window — each FIA cycle is effectively a controlled growth experiment at landscape scale. This makes the FIA cycle the natural empirical anchor against which LANDIS-II productivity parameters should be calibrated and against which projections should be validated.

We propose here a structured calibration ladder, anchored in multi-cycle FIA hindcast skill, and apply it across three states whose forests differ in nearly every ecologically relevant respect. Maine's untreated FIA plots are dominated by a spruce-fir matrix subject to recurring spruce budworm outbreaks; Georgia's are dominated by southern pine systems on a much shorter biomass-accumulation horizon under recurrent prescribed fire; Washington's are dominated by Pacific Northwest conifers spanning a steep coastal-to-interior productivity gradient. If a single calibration framework works across these three contexts, we argue it will work in the dozens of regions in between.

The ladder we propose comprises four tiers of increasing parameter complexity. Tier 0 is the uncalibrated literature baseline. Tier 1 applies a single uniform multiplier θ to all ANPPmax and BiomassMax values across all species and ecoregions; this collapses the entire bias correction into one number and tests whether the systematic component of the misfit can be expressed as a single productivity rescaling. Tier 1.5 allows θ to vary by ecoregion. Tier 2 allows the multiplier to vary by species, optimized via Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolutionary Strategy (CMA-ES; Hansen 2006) directly against the FIA hindcast log-likelihood. Higher tiers risk overfitting the limited FIA observations and are not pursued in this work.

For each tier, calibration skill is evaluated using a log-likelihood derived from log-residuals between LANDIS-predicted and FIA-observed plot biomass at every FIA remeasurement year. Each plot's initial conditions are set from its first FIA measurement (year `FIRST_INVYR`, which varies by plot from 2001 to 2010), and the LANDIS run is then projected forward for the remainder of the inventory cycle. Each subsequent FIA remeasurement is paired against the LANDIS prediction at the matching calendar year. This forward-projection scheme provides a fair multi-cycle hindcast in the absence of pre-2001 tree-list data: we let the model start at the same year FIA started, then ask whether it tracks reality over the next two decades. With roughly 14,000 untreated plots across the three states, the resulting paired dataset contains over 25,000 (LANDIS-prediction, FIA-observation) pairs — substantially more than the validation data underpinning most published regional LANDIS-II applications.

Three concrete questions drive the analysis. First, are the uncalibrated literature parameters systematically biased, and if so, in which direction across our three states? Second, what calibration tier suffices to remove the bias — does the systematic component reduce to a single multiplier, or does eliminating it require species-by-species adjustment? And third, does the calibrated model's century-horizon biomass asymptote differ meaningfully from the uncalibrated case in ways that affect downstream policy-relevant uses such as forest carbon accounting? Answering these questions requires the calibration framework we describe in the rest of this paper. Once answered, the calibrated LANDIS-II becomes a defensible substrate for the climate-by-harvest-by-disturbance projection studies the model was designed for.
