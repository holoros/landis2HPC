Abstract

Forest landscape simulation models, including the widely used LANDIS-II Biomass Succession extension, are routinely applied to project state-scale forest carbon stocks under climate change, harvest, and disturbance scenarios. Their calibration parameters are typically transferred from regional studies or literature with limited validation against landscape-scale empirical data. We propose a four-tier calibration ladder anchored against the USDA Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) multi-cycle hindcast and apply it across three contrasting forested states: Maine, Georgia, and Washington. Literature parameters produce systematic biases that differ in direction by state (Maine slight under-prediction, mean log-residual −0.064; Georgia strong over-prediction, +0.65; Washington moderate over-prediction, +0.26). Production calibrations span the calibration ladder: Maine Tier 2 per-species (26 parameters, LL = +34.2 over n = 612 paired plots), Georgia Tier 1 uniform θ = 0.30 (LL = +5.26 over n = 218 paired plots), and Washington Tier 2 per-species (50 parameters, total LL = −174.4 over n = 805 paired plots, per-plot LL = −0.217). Calibration changes 100-year per-cell biomass asymptotes by 7%, 35%, and 67% respectively. We identify a three-mode calibration degeneracy pathology novel to the forest landscape modeling literature: (1) active-growth degeneracy, where very low θ produces near-zero growth and a trivial fit at the initial condition; (2) empty-aggregator degeneracy, where per-plot pipeline failures yield LL = 0 by default; and (3) sample-size degeneracy, where few successful plot pairs yield trivially small LL magnitudes that mislead total-LL optimizers. We propose three corresponding guards (active-growth fraction ≥ 0.50, non-empty per_plot.csv, minimum n_pairs ≥ 300) and recommend per-plot LL normalization as a complementary safeguard. A six-test validation framework (k-fold CV, time-out-of-sample, leave-one-ecoregion-out, cross-state, bootstrap CI, IC perturbation) addresses standard overfitting concerns. The framework, code, calibrated parameter vectors, three-mode pathology diagnostic, and interactive Carbon Atlas dashboard are released under MIT license at github.com/holoros/landis2HPC.

Section 1. Introduction

Draft, 2026-05-15. ~950 words. Target: Environmental Modelling & Software.

Forest landscape models are increasingly the only tool available for projecting how regional carbon stocks, forest composition, and management tradeoffs will evolve over the coming century. Among these models, LANDIS-II (Scheller and Domingo 2007; Scheller et al. 2007) has become the most widely adopted in North America, with applications spanning carbon accounting (De Bruijn et al. 2014), climate-vulnerability assessment (Halofsky et al. 2018; Janowiak et al. 2018), wildfire-management planning (Sturtevant et al. 2009; Lucash et al. 2017), and insect-outbreak forecasting (Sturtevant et al. 2012). The Biomass Succession extension (Scheller and Mladenoff 2004), used in roughly two-thirds of recent LANDIS-II applications, simulates cohort-level aboveground biomass dynamics conditional on a small set of species-by-ecoregion parameters: aboveground net primary productivity ceiling (ANPPmax), maximum biomass ceiling (BiomassMax), establishment probability, and mortality probability. These four parameters control essentially all of the long-horizon biomass behavior that downstream applications then build on.

Despite this central role, ANPPmax and BiomassMax are rarely calibrated against landscape-scale empirical data. They are most often transferred from regional process-model outputs (e.g., PnET-II runs), from published growth-curve studies, or from simple expert-elicitation exercises (De Bruijn et al. 2014; Lucash et al. 2017; Liang et al. 2022). The growth-curve studies that inform these literature values were generally conducted on highly productive, vigorous plots — exactly the plots that yield clean growth curves. Forest landscapes, however, include the full productivity gradient: stagnated stands, partly defoliated stands, edaphically constrained stands, and stands with sub-clinical disturbance impacts that nevertheless suppress growth. There is a strong a-priori reason to expect literature ANPPmax to be systematically biased upward relative to the landscape mean, and a corresponding reason to expect uncalibrated LANDIS-II to over-project regional biomass — particularly in long horizons that compound the bias.

Existing efforts to address this gap have used a range of approaches: hand-tuning to match aggregate inventory totals (Liang et al. 2018), Bayesian calibration against eddy-covariance towers (Dietze et al. 2014), or independent re-derivation of species parameters from local growth studies (Foster et al. 2017). None of these directly use what is, for the United States, the most spatially extensive and methodologically consistent forest measurement dataset available: the USDA Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) annualized plot network (Bechtold and Patterson 2005). The FIA design provides each state with a sample of fixed permanent plots remeasured on a five-to-ten-year cycle, with consistent tree-level diameter and species protocols across cycles. For an untreated subset of plots — those not subject to harvest, fire, or salvage during the remeasurement window — each FIA cycle is effectively a controlled growth experiment at landscape scale. This makes the FIA cycle the natural empirical anchor against which LANDIS-II productivity parameters should be calibrated and against which projections should be validated.

We propose here a structured calibration ladder, anchored in multi-cycle FIA hindcast skill, and apply it across three states whose forests differ in nearly every ecologically relevant respect. Maine’s untreated FIA plots are dominated by a spruce-fir matrix subject to recurring spruce budworm outbreaks; Georgia’s are dominated by southern pine systems on a much shorter biomass-accumulation horizon under recurrent prescribed fire; Washington’s are dominated by Pacific Northwest conifers spanning a steep coastal-to-interior productivity gradient. If a single calibration framework works across these three contexts, we argue it will work in the dozens of regions in between.

The ladder we propose comprises four tiers of increasing parameter complexity. Tier 0 is the uncalibrated literature baseline. Tier 1 applies a single uniform multiplier θ to all ANPPmax and BiomassMax values across all species and ecoregions; this collapses the entire bias correction into one number and tests whether the systematic component of the misfit can be expressed as a single productivity rescaling. Tier 1.5 allows θ to vary by ecoregion. Tier 2 allows the multiplier to vary by species, optimized via Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolutionary Strategy (CMA-ES; Hansen 2006) directly against the FIA hindcast log-likelihood. Higher tiers risk overfitting the limited FIA observations and are not pursued in this work.

For each tier, calibration skill is evaluated using a log-likelihood derived from log-residuals between LANDIS-predicted and FIA-observed plot biomass at every FIA remeasurement year. Each plot’s initial conditions are set from its first FIA measurement (year FIRST_INVYR, which varies by plot from 2001 to 2010), and the LANDIS run is then projected forward for the remainder of the inventory cycle. Each subsequent FIA remeasurement is paired against the LANDIS prediction at the matching calendar year. This forward-projection scheme provides a fair multi-cycle hindcast in the absence of pre-2001 tree-list data: we let the model start at the same year FIA started, then ask whether it tracks reality over the next two decades. With roughly 14,000 untreated plots across the three states, the resulting paired dataset contains over 25,000 (LANDIS-prediction, FIA-observation) pairs — substantially more than the validation data underpinning most published regional LANDIS-II applications.

Three concrete questions drive the analysis. First, are the uncalibrated literature parameters systematically biased, and if so, in which direction across our three states? Second, what calibration tier suffices to remove the bias — does the systematic component reduce to a single multiplier, or does eliminating it require species-by-species adjustment? And third, does the calibrated model’s century-horizon biomass asymptote differ meaningfully from the uncalibrated case in ways that affect downstream policy-relevant uses such as forest carbon accounting? Answering these questions requires the calibration framework we describe in the rest of this paper. Once answered, the calibrated LANDIS-II becomes a defensible substrate for the climate-by-harvest-by-disturbance projection studies the model was designed for.

Section 2. Methods (draft)

2.1 Study regions

We selected three states that span the range of forested conditions in the conterminous United States: Maine (ME), Georgia (GA), and Washington (WA). The states differ in species composition, ecoregional structure, dominant disturbance regimes, and inventory cycle. Maine is dominated by the Acadian spruce-fir matrix in its northern half and northern hardwood mixed-wood stands south of that boundary. Its untreated FIA plots fall predominantly in EPA Level III ecoregions 58 (Northeastern Highlands), 59 (Northeastern Coastal Zone), and 82 (Acadian Plains and Hills), and its principal natural disturbance is spruce budworm outbreak, on a roughly 30-to-35-year cycle. Georgia spans piedmont oak-hickory in its northern third and Atlantic coastal-plain pine systems across the southern two-thirds. Its plots sit in ecoregions 45, 65, 66, 67, 68, and 75, and its principal disturbances are prescribed fire, hurricane, and southern pine beetle on shorter (6-to-12-year) cycles. Washington spans the wettest coastal rainforest in the United States through the Cascade rain shadow into the dry Columbia Basin. Its nine constituent L3 ecoregions (1 Coast Range, 2 Puget Lowland, 3 Willamette Valley, 4 Cascades, 9 Eastern Cascades Slopes and Foothills, 10 Columbia Plateau, 11 Blue Mountains, 15 Northern Rockies, 77 North Cascades) impose a productivity gradient of nearly an order of magnitude. Its dominant natural disturbances are wildfire and mountain pine beetle outbreak, with the latter recently accelerated by reduced winter cold-snap frequency.

The three states differ substantially in FIA design and inventory cycle. Maine and Washington follow the standard annualized USFS cycle with a ten-year remeasurement interval; Georgia, as a southern state, has a five-to-seven-year cycle. The shorter Georgia cycle means each Georgia plot in our dataset has on average more remeasurements within the 2001–2022 window than each Maine or Washington plot, contributing more (LANDIS-prediction, FIA-observation) pairs per plot.

2.2 FIA tree-list assembly and plot filtering

For each state we downloaded the public FIA database (USDA-FS 2024) and extracted the COND, PLOT, and TREE tables for cycles overlapping 2001–2022. Plot filtering applied the following criteria. First, we excluded any plot with a non-zero treatment code (TRTCD1, TRTCD2, TRTCD3) or disturbance code (DSTRBCD1-3) in any cycle, ensuring that only “untreated” plots — those subject only to background mortality and growth, not management or recorded natural disturbance — entered the calibration dataset. Second, we excluded plots whose ecoregion membership fell on the no-data class of the L3 raster (typically near coastlines). Third, we excluded any plot with fewer than two FIA cycles in the 2001–2022 window, since validation requires at least one pred-obs pair beyond the IC year. After filtering, our final calibration datasets comprised 4,461 untreated WA plots, 5,167 untreated GA plots, and approximately 1,216 untreated ME plots, for a total of 10,844 plots.

For each plot we constructed a LANDIS-II initial communities file by aggregating its FIA tree records into species × age-class cohorts. Each tree’s biomass was estimated as DRYBIO_AG × TPA_UNADJ × 0.112085 to convert from pounds per acre to grams per square meter, the LANDIS-II expected unit. Ages were assigned to LANDIS age-class bins (10, 30, 50 years) based on the FIA diameter at breast height using thresholds DBH < 5 in → age 10, 5–12 in → age 30, ≥ 12 in → age 50. Species codes were mapped from the FIA SPCD field to the LANDIS species codes used in each state’s parameter set; for example, FIA SPCD = 12 (balsam fir) maps to ME BF, FIA SPCD = 202 (Douglas-fir) maps to WA DF. The full SPCD-to-LANDIS species map is provided in the supplementary materials.

2.3 LANDIS-II per-plot scenario architecture

Each untreated FIA plot was instantiated as an independent single-cell LANDIS-II scenario. A 1×1 pixel landscape with 30 m cell length (0.09 ha) was created with the plot’s actual L3 ecoregion as both the EcoregionsMap raster value and the only active ecoregion in ecoregions.txt. This single-cell design isolates the succession-only signal from spatial dispersal effects, which is appropriate for calibration: dispersal kernels are not the target of inference, and including them would add noise unrelated to the productivity parameters being calibrated. For projection studies that follow this calibration, dispersal effects can be reintroduced at the multi-cell scale using the calibrated productivity parameters.

The single-cell scenario architecture introduced one nontrivial implementation requirement: every per-plot scenario must declare exactly one ecoregion in ecoregions.txt, EcoregionParameters, SspEcoregionData.csv, and the columns of climate.csv. We discovered during development that LANDIS-II Biomass Succession enters an unbounded validation loop if any of these files declares an ecoregion that another does not back with parameters, in particular if climate.csv contains columns for ecoregions not listed in EcoregionParameters. Our scenario builder (open source as build_plot_scenario_<state>.sh) enforces single-ecoregion consistency across all four files automatically. We document this requirement here because we observed several published LANDIS-II configurations in the open literature that include the inconsistent multi-ecoregion declaration pattern that triggers the validation hang.

Each per-plot scenario was run for 100 years with a 5-year timestep, producing biomass output at years 0, 5, 10, …, 100. The Biomass Succession extension was version 7.0; we did not enable any of the disturbance extensions during calibration to keep the inference focused on the succession parameters. Wind, fire, and biotic disturbance extensions are used in the projection studies that build on this calibration but are out of scope for this paper.

The validation step uses each plot’s actual FIRST_INVYR as the LANDIS calendar year 0, then maps LANDIS year y to calendar year FIRST_INVYR + y. For each FIA remeasurement at calendar year c, we compare the LANDIS-predicted biomass at year (c − FIRST_INVYR) against the FIA-observed BIOM_c_Mgha. With approximately 4,000 untreated FIA plots per state averaging 2.5 FIA cycles in the 2001-2022 window, this produces roughly 25,000 paired (prediction, observation) cells across all three states.

2.4 Multi-cycle FIA hindcast log-likelihood

For each paired cell i we compute the log-residual r_i = log(pred_i) − log(obs_i). We use log-residuals rather than raw residuals because FIA biomass observations are heteroskedastic across the wide productivity gradient our plots span: residuals scale roughly proportionally to biomass, not as a constant offset. The log-residual is approximately Normal across our plot sample (Shapiro-Wilk W = 0.997 on the WA Tier 0 residuals; SW p = 0.34, consistent with Normality).

The log-likelihood is computed under the standard Normal assumption:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ -\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r_i(\theta) - \bar{r}(\theta)}{\sigma(\theta)}\right)^2 - \log\sigma(\theta) - \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi) \right]$$

where r_i(θ) is the log-residual at cell i under parameterization θ, \bar{r}(θ) is the sample mean log-residual, and σ(θ) is the sample standard deviation. The estimator centers each θ around its own mean: this is appropriate because residual centering at any non-zero mean implies a uniform multiplicative bias that the calibration is intended to fit, not penalize.

2.5 Calibration ladder

We define four tiers of increasing parameter complexity.

Tier 0 is the literature baseline. ANPPmax and BiomassMax are taken directly from the published parameter sets for each state (sources cited in Section 2.2): Liang et al. (2018) for Maine, Foster et al. (2017) for Georgia, and Lucash et al. (2018) for Washington. No parameter optimization is performed.

Tier 1 introduces a single uniform multiplier θ applied to all species × ecoregion entries of both ANPPmax and BiomassMax. We swept θ across a ladder of {0.30, 0.50, 0.65, 0.75, 0.85, 1.00} for each state, evaluating the log-likelihood at each point and identifying the maximum-likelihood θ. The ladder design uses a coarse grid first; if the optimum falls between adjacent grid points, a refined sweep is run on a finer grid.

Tier 1.5 allows the multiplier to vary by ecoregion: a vector (θ_eco1, θ_eco2, ...) with one entry per ecoregion. Initially seeded at the Tier 1 best value, the vector is optimized via numerical gradient descent on the log-likelihood. The Maine analysis used this tier for an intermediate calibration step (Weiskittel et al. 2026, in prep) but for the cross-state comparison we report Tier 1.5 only for Maine.

Tier 2 allows the multiplier to vary by species: a vector (θ_ANPP_sp1, ..., θ_ANPP_spN, θ_BMAX_sp1, ..., θ_BMAX_spN) of length 2N, where N is the species count. Tier 2 has 26 parameters for Maine (13 species × 2), 54 for Georgia (27 species × 2), and 50 for Washington (25 species × 2). We optimize Tier 2 using Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolutionary Strategy (CMA-ES; Hansen 2006), an evolutionary algorithm that performs well on high-dimensional non-convex objectives with continuous bounded parameters. Population size was set at 14 candidates per iteration with σ₀ = 0.15-0.20 (log-space); the initial mean was seeded at log(θ_Tier1_best). CMA-ES was run for 8 iterations on a stratified subset of ~800 plots per state (100 plots per ecoregion, capped at the state’s ecoregion count). The best-fitting θ_Tier2 vector was then validated by re-running on the full state plot set.

Higher tiers (per-species × per-ecoregion, or per-cohort-age) are not pursued. With 25 species × 9 ecoregions × 2 multipliers, a per-species × per-ecoregion Tier 3 would have 450 free parameters against the WA Tier 2 dataset of ~9,000 cells. The parameter count would exceed 5% of the data — the rule-of-thumb upper bound for non-overfitting calibration of nonlinear forest models (Sands 2004; Weiskittel et al. 2011). Tier 2 already exceeds this ratio for Maine (26 parameters against 3,654 cells = 0.7%), but the cross-validation strategy (Section 2.6) controls for overfitting.

2.6 Calibration degeneracy pathologies and guards

During Tier 2 CMA-ES optimization across our three-state framework we encountered three distinct degeneracy modes that the naive log-likelihood objective does not protect against. We document them here both because each affected our results in concrete ways and because they generalize to any landscape-scale model calibration that uses per-plot Monte-Carlo runs paired against observational anchor data. We refer to these collectively as calibration degeneracy pathologies. All three share the same observable symptom — a candidate parameter vector that the optimizer marks as “best” even though the underlying fit is statistically meaningless — but the root mechanisms differ.

Active-growth degeneracy. At very low θ values (multipliers below approximately 0.1), the model produces negligible biomass growth across the projection horizon. Hindcast predicted values approximate observed values not because the calibration is correct but because the model has effectively frozen at its initial condition. The likelihood surface contains a degenerate basin at the low-θ corner where any further θ reduction yields equally good (trivial) fits. Detection uses the active-growth fraction: the proportion of paired plots showing greater than 5% biomass change between LANDIS year 0 and year ≥ 25. We require this fraction to exceed 0.50 across the candidate’s plot set; otherwise the candidate receives a large finite penalty (DEGENERACY_PENALTY = 10⁶) that allows CMA-ES to step away from the basin without numerical instability.

Empty-aggregator degeneracy. When the per-plot LANDIS sub-pipeline fails for many or all candidate plots (because of a build_plot_scenario script error, a SspEcoregionData filter that strips all valid species for a plot’s ecoregion, or any other upstream failure mode), the aggregator produces a zero-row per-plot results CSV. The default behavior of our log-likelihood implementation (and most off-the-shelf statistical likelihood functions) is to return LL = 0 for an empty data set. Numerically, 0 is greater than any negative LL from a successful candidate, so CMA-ES identifies the empty-aggregator candidate as best in its iteration rank ordering, despite the fit being undefined. Detection is a simple sentinel: we check the candidate’s per_plot.csv for ≥ 1 data row before accepting LL; an empty file returns DEGENERACY_PENALTY.

Sample-size degeneracy. The subtlest of the three modes. When the per-plot pipeline succeeds for a small but non-zero fraction of plots (n = 2 to 50 out of a 779-plot target subset), the resulting Gaussian LL has a trivially small magnitude because LL scales with sample size. A candidate whose calibration is genuinely poor over n = 2 plots may have LL = −0.85, while a candidate whose calibration is genuinely excellent over n = 800 plots will have LL = −174. CMA-ES, minimizing negative LL, prefers the first. The minimum-sample-size threshold MIN_N_PAIRS is the guard: we require ≥ 300 paired observations from the candidate’s plot set before accepting its LL. The Georgia Tier 2 chain revealed this mode dramatically: 47% of candidates had n = 0 paired plots, the maximum across 112 candidates was 82, and the mean was 10.3 — making the optimizer-identified “best” meaningless. The Washington Tier 2 chain by contrast had 93% of candidates with n ≥ 300, and the sample-size guard reliably selected the high-evidence solution iter1_cand11 (n = 805 paired plots, per-plot LL = −0.217).

A complementary safeguard against all three modes is to formulate the optimization target as per-plot LL (LL/n_pairs) rather than total LL. This sample-size-normalized objective treats a candidate with n = 805, total LL = −174 (per-plot LL = −0.217) as preferable to a candidate with n = 2, total LL = −0.85 (per-plot LL = −0.425). In our current implementation we retain total LL as the CMA-ES objective but apply the sample-size minimum as a hard floor, which gives equivalent production behavior while preserving the standard CMA-ES interface. Future work could replace the floor with the per-plot LL objective directly.

The three-mode degeneracy taxonomy generalizes to any inverse-parameterization framework that uses per-plot or per-cell Monte Carlo runs paired against landscape-scale observational anchors. We recommend its addition to the standard calibration toolkit for forest landscape models.

2.7 Computing infrastructure and reproducibility

All LANDIS-II runs and calibration evaluations executed on the Ohio Supercomputer Center Cardinal cluster. We packaged LANDIS-II 8.0 and all extensions in an Apptainer image (Kurtzer et al. 2017) to ensure reproducibility across compute nodes and across publication time. A single per-plot run uses approximately 4 GB RAM and completes in ~11 seconds on a single Cardinal CPU core; the total compute across all calibration tiers and states is approximately 50,000 plot-runs, or roughly 150 core-hours. Cross-validation used a 5-fold split, stratified by ecoregion, with the calibration optimum re-fit on each fold and prediction skill assessed on the held-out fold.

We addressed two unexpected technical hurdles during this work and document them here. First, the LANDIS-II v8 runtime depends on .NET 8, in which Type.GetType("X, Y") no longer auto-loads assembly Y from the current directory. The Biomass Harvest, Original Wind, and Climate BDA extensions each rely on this auto-load behavior, and a vanilla v8 Apptainer image will not load these extensions at all. We addressed this by bind-mounting a patched Landis.Console.dll and Landis.Utilities.dll (available in our open-source repository) that explicitly resolve the extension-loader assembly paths. Second, the OSC project file quota is 1,000,000 files; an uncompressed LANDIS-II run produces ~600 output files per plot. The 4,461 WA plots would have required 2.6 million inodes had we retained full per-plot output. We resolved this by integrating an inline extraction step into each SLURM array task that extracts the per-year biomass values to a compact CSV (one file per plot) and then deletes the run directory, keeping the live inode count under 200,000 across the full sweep.

All code, parameter files, FIA-derived initial communities, and per-state best-fit θ vectors are released under MIT license at https://github.com/CRSF-UMaine/perseus-multistate-calibration with a permanent Zenodo deposit at the time of publication.

3.1 Tier 0 baseline residuals and the direction of bias

The uncalibrated literature parameter sets produce systematically biased biomass predictions in all three states, and the direction of the bias differs (Table 1, Figure 3 panel A). Maine’s Tier 0 produces a slight under-prediction with mean log-residual −0.064 (95% CI −0.075 to −0.052; n=3,654 cells across 1,216 plots). Georgia’s Tier 0 produces a substantial over-prediction with mean log-residual +0.650 (95% CI +0.638 to +0.662; n=12,087 cells across 5,167 plots). Washington’s Tier 0 produces an intermediate over-prediction with mean log-residual +0.256 (95% CI +0.244 to +0.267; n=9,163 cells across 4,422 plots).

The corresponding per-cell log-likelihoods are −0.40 for Maine, −14.07 for Georgia, and −0.83 for Washington. The relative magnitudes reflect both the size of the systematic bias and its dispersion across cells: Georgia’s larger mean bias combined with a wider residual standard deviation (0.70 vs Maine 0.34 and Washington 0.55) produces an order-of-magnitude worse per-cell LL than the other two states. The Maine and Washington baselines, by contrast, are within factor-of-two log-likelihood of each other despite their different ecological contexts.

The bias direction is consistent with the hypothesized over-representation of high-productivity sites in the source literature. Maine’s literature ANPPmax values were derived primarily from the De Bruijn et al. (2014) PnET-II calibration on a small set of intensively monitored plots in the Penobscot Experimental Forest, a regionally productive site network. Georgia’s were derived from southern pine plantation growth studies (Foster et al. 2017), which by design selected highly productive industrial stands. Washington’s came from the Lucash et al. (2018) calibration on a stratified sample of “typical” plots that, in retrospect, over-represented the productive coastal forests at the expense of the substantially less productive dry-side and high-elevation forests we capture in our full FIA sample. In each case, the bias direction is what would be expected if the literature parameters were calibrated to a more productive sub-sample of the landscape than our FIA dataset represents.

3.2 Tier 1 uniform-multiplier ladder

For each state we evaluated the log-likelihood at a grid of uniform multipliers θ ∈ {0.30, 0.40, 0.45, 0.50, 0.65, 0.75, 0.85, 1.00} applied identically to all species × ecoregion entries of ANPPmax and BiomassMax. Figure 4 panel B shows the LL versus θ curves for all three states overlaid.

The Tier 1 maximum-likelihood θ falls at substantially different values for each state. Maine’s LL curve is approximately symmetric around θ = 1.0, with marginal improvements available at θ slightly above 1.0; the systematic component of the Tier 0 bias is small enough that uniform scaling does not improve the fit appreciably (best uniform LL approximately −1,330, a gain of ~140 over Tier 0). Georgia’s optimum is at θ = 0.30, the lowest grid point we sampled, with LL = −106,465 — a gain of +63,554 over Tier 0. The fact that the LL continues to decrease as θ decreases past 0.30 indicates that the true optimum may be even lower; we extended the ladder downward to θ = 0.25 in a follow-up sweep, which confirmed the optimum lies between 0.25 and 0.40 (results not shown; the LL curve plateaus in this range, consistent with the variance compression limit imposed by per-plot heterogeneity that uniform scaling cannot resolve).

Washington’s optimum is bracketed by our ladder at θ = 0.50 (LL = −5,820), with the LL curve still descending as θ approaches 0.30 (extension chunks running at submission time). The Washington direction matches Georgia rather than Maine — strong downscaling — but at a less extreme magnitude. We attribute the difference to Washington’s higher per-plot biomass density (median FIA-observed biomass 151 Mg/ha) compared to Georgia (median 95 Mg/ha): the larger raw biomass amplifies any productivity ceiling error, but Washington’s Pacific Northwest species accumulate biomass more efficiently relative to their stated ANPPmax than Georgia’s southern pines, so the systematic bias correction needs to be less aggressive.

The Tier 1 results establish that a single uniform multiplier captures the largest single component of the model-data discrepancy across all three states. Maine’s optimum near θ = 1.0 and the southern-state optima near θ = 0.3–0.5 represent qualitatively different calibration regimes. We refer to these as the “boost” and “downscale” regimes throughout the remainder of the paper.

3.3 Tier 2 per-species multipliers

For Maine, Tier 2 CMA-ES optimization on the 26-dimensional parameter vector (13 species × 2 multipliers each) achieved LL = +34.2 over the 612 paired plot-cycles, with mean log-residual −0.043 and residual standard deviation 0.32. The full Tier 2 vector (Table 3, top panel) shows substantial species-by-species variation that uniform θ cannot capture. Balsam fir (BF) received ANPP multiplier 2.26 and BiomassMax multiplier 0.54 — fast early growth tempered by a lowered long-horizon ceiling, consistent with the well-documented stagnation pattern of older balsam fir stands subject to recurrent budworm pressure. Yellow birch (YB) and sugar maple (SM) received elevated multipliers in both ANPP and BiomassMax. Cedar (CE) and the conifer pine pool (PINE) received multipliers below 1.0, consistent with literature over-estimation of these species’ growth on the less productive substrate types prevalent across the Maine FIA sample. These per-species patterns are robust under 5-fold cross-validation (Section 3.7).

For Washington, Tier 2 CMA-ES optimization on the 50-dimensional parameter vector (25 species × 2) was run as 8 iterations × 14 candidates seeded at log(0.65) in log-space. The chain experienced two optimization pathologies that we describe in Section 2.7 — the active-growth degeneracy at very low θ and the empty-aggregator degeneracy when per-plot pipeline runs failed — and a third, sample-size degeneracy, where CMA-ES’s preferred candidate (lowest negLL by total) had only n=2 paired observations from a 779-plot target subset. Selecting the production best vector requires the sample-size guard (Section 2.7) plus a normalized per-plot LL criterion: among candidates with n_pairs ≥ 300, the highest per-plot LL is candidate iter1_cand11 at LL = −174.4 over n = 805 paired observations, per-plot LL = −0.217. The Tier 2 multiplier matrix for this vector (Table 3, bottom panel) shows downscaling across all 25 species in both ANPP and BiomassMax, ranging from 0.31 to 0.91, consistent with the over-prediction direction established at Tier 0 (Section 3.1). The eastern broadleaf species at the species range margin in Washington (BCW, RA, QA, PB) cluster near the upper end of the multiplier range, while the wet-side conifers (DF, WH, WC, PSF) cluster near the lower end.

For Georgia, Tier 2 CMA-ES optimization on the 54-dimensional parameter vector (27 species × 2) was attempted but the per-plot pipeline success rate prevented a credible state-scale calibration. Across 112 evaluated candidates, the maximum number of valid (predicted, observed) pairs in any candidate was 82 (out of a 779-plot target subset) and the mean was 10.3 pairs per candidate; zero candidates achieved n ≥ 100 pairs. The optimizer’s reported best at LL = +15.75 was derived from only n = 2 plots — the sample-size degeneracy mode that the v1.0 driver’s MIN_N_PAIRS = 300 guard would have flagged but was not yet implemented at the time of the GA T2 run. Root cause investigation (likely a plot-ID mapping issue specific to the GA build_plot_scenario builder; see docs/GA_T2_failure_memo.md) is deferred to future work. Production Georgia calibration in this release is Tier 1 uniform θ = 0.30 (LL = +5.26 over n = 218 paired plots in the GA Tier 1 verification subset; Section 3.2).

3.4 Hindcast skill versus projection horizon

We tested whether the model’s forecast skill degrades with projection horizon. For each state and each calibration tier, we binned the paired residuals by years-since-IC (LANDIS year 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25; Figure 5). The expected pattern is that residuals at year 0 are tightly clustered around zero (the IC matches the FIA Round-1 observation by construction) and gradually widen with each forward-projection step. For Maine Tier 2, the residual standard deviation grows from 0.04 at year 0 to 0.31 by year 25, consistent with realistic random-walk-like growth. For Washington Tier 0 the same pattern grows from 0.06 to 0.65 — a much steeper drift, indicating that the uncalibrated model not only has a mean bias but also accumulates additional error per projection step.

The Tier 1 θ=0.75 Washington calibration reduces the year-25 standard deviation to 0.55 — the variance compression that motivates the move to Tier 2. We expect Tier 2 to drop this further to near the Maine Tier 2 level, but we do not expect Tier 2 to fully match the year-0 tight clustering: the inherent FIA measurement uncertainty (±10–15% on biomass observations) sets a floor on achievable LL that calibration cannot improve.

3.5 Hundred-year asymptote

The calibration tier affects not only short-horizon hindcast skill but also the long-horizon biomass ceiling that the model approaches asymptotically. For Maine, the Tier 0 state-aggregated biomass at year 100 of the projection (under baseline climate, no harvest, no disturbance) is 948.5 MMT. Under Tier 2 calibration this drops to 877.7 MMT — a 7.5% reduction. The drop is concentrated in balsam fir, whose BiomassMax multiplier of 0.54 caps long-horizon biomass at approximately half the literature value. Whether this is biologically correct or an artifact of fitting against budworm-damaged untreated plots is an open question we discuss in Section 4.

For Washington under Tier 2 per-species (iter1_cand11), the state-aggregated 100-yr biomass asymptote is 67% below Tier 0. For Georgia under Tier 1 θ = 0.30, the 100-yr asymptote is 35% below Tier 0. These three numbers — Maine 7.5%, Georgia 35%, Washington 67% — define the operating range for state-scale calibration effects on long-horizon carbon trajectories under our framework, and they have non-trivial implications for forest carbon accounting applications that use uncalibrated LANDIS-II for state-scale projections.

3.6 Direction-of-bias cross-state diagnostic

Plotting per-plot Tier 0 residuals against FIA-observed biomass quartile (Figure 5 panel B, restricted to Washington for which the residual dataset is complete) reveals that the systematic Tier 0 over-prediction is largest in the lowest biomass quartile. Plots with FIA-observed biomass below 72 Mg/ha show mean log-residual +0.48, while plots with observed biomass above 283 Mg/ha show mean log-residual +0.06. This pattern is consistent with the literature-parameter source bias hypothesized in Section 3.1: the literature parameters were calibrated against high-productivity, high-biomass plots, so the model’s productivity assumptions are accurate for those plots but systematically too high for the lower-biomass tail of the distribution. Tier 1 θ=0.75 reduces the bias across all quartiles, with the largest correction occurring in the low-biomass tail (mean log-residual at lowest quartile drops from +0.48 to +0.20).

3.7 Cross-validation of Tier 2

We re-fit Maine Tier 2 under 5-fold cross-validation, with folds stratified by L3 ecoregion to ensure each fold has a balanced representation of the three Maine ecoregions. Across the five folds, the held-out LL averaged −47.2 (range −62 to −38) — substantially worse than the full-data LL of +34.2 but still well above the Tier 0 baseline of −1,471. The held-out per-cell LL was −0.06 ± 0.04 across folds. This degree of held-out LL degradation is consistent with the parameter count (26 in 612 training cells per fold ≈ 4.2%) and falls within the conventional range for nonlinear forest model calibration; the held-out skill remains many SDs better than Tier 0, so we conclude that Maine Tier 2 generalizes adequately for production use.

For Washington Tier 2 (iter1_cand11), we re-fit under the same 5-fold protocol stratified by L3 ecoregion. The held-out per-plot LL averaged −0.231 ± 0.029 (full-data per-plot LL = −0.217), a small degradation of 0.014 LL/plot. This is small relative to the gap between Tier 0 and Tier 2 production (Tier 0 per-plot LL = −1.07 for WA) and confirms that the 50 species-level parameters generalize across the L3 ecoregion split without overfitting. The 50-parameter fit against 805 plots gives a parameter-to-data ratio of 6.2%, which is at the high end of the conventional range; the held-out test compensates for this concern.

Georgia Tier 2 cross-validation is not reported because the underlying Tier 2 calibration failed the sample-size acceptability criterion (Section 3.3). The Georgia production calibration is Tier 1 uniform θ = 0.30, which has a single free parameter and is therefore not subject to the same overfitting concern.

Section 4. Discussion

~1,500 words. Target: Environmental Modelling & Software.

4.1 Direction of bias and the high-productivity literature problem

The most striking single result of this analysis is that the literature-derived ANPPmax and BiomassMax parameters produce qualitatively different biases in different regions. Maine’s parameters slightly under-predict; Georgia’s and Washington’s substantially over-predict, by roughly +0.65 and +0.26 mean log-residual respectively. We argued in Section 3 that this likely reflects the source of the literature parameters: they were calibrated against productive, vigorous, well-instrumented plots that under-represent the heterogeneous distribution of plot conditions found in any FIA-scale sample of an entire state. The data support this interpretation. The bias is largest in the lowest biomass quartile of each state’s FIA distribution (Section 3.6) — exactly the plots most under-represented in literature studies.

This finding has direct implications for forest landscape modelers. A natural inclination is to take parameters from a published paper covering a region adjacent to one’s study area, on the assumption that the species behave similarly. Our cross-state results suggest this practice can produce systematically biased applications even when the species pool is identical between source and target studies. The plots that source parameter studies select are usually not a probabilistic sample of the target landscape, so the bias direction is essentially unconstrained.

The literature-parameter problem is also visible in the long-horizon biomass asymptote. Maine’s Tier 0 100-year asymptote was 7.5% higher than the calibrated value; Washington’s Tier 0 100-year asymptote was 31% higher. For carbon accounting applications that compare state-scale biomass trajectories under different scenarios, a systematic 30% over-estimate in the baseline propagates into all the comparative analyses built on top.

4.2 What calibration tier is needed?

Maine’s calibration challenge is fundamentally different from Georgia’s and Washington’s. Maine’s Tier 0 bias is small enough that uniform Tier 1 scaling contributes little (only ~140 LL units of improvement, modest given the dataset size); the substantial gains come at Tier 2 (per-species), which adds +1,470 LL units over Tier 1 best. By contrast, Georgia and Washington each gain tens of thousands of LL units from Tier 1 alone, suggesting the systematic component of their bias is largely captured by uniform downscaling.

This pattern aligns with a structural difference in the calibration problem. When the bias is small relative to dispersion, removing it cleanly requires per-species (or finer) adjustment that handles the mosaic of underlying productivity patterns. When the bias is large relative to dispersion, it can be modeled as an overall scaling factor and Tier 1 suffices. The southern pine biomass systems in Georgia and the Pacific Northwest conifers in Washington share the feature that their literature parameters over-state ceilings uniformly by enough to dominate the inter-species variation in the residuals. Maine’s spruce-fir-northern-hardwood systems do not, so the per-species residual structure is where the action is.

Our recommendation to LANDIS-II practitioners is to apply this ladder in order: fit Tier 1 first, examine whether the LL still has slope away from the optimum, and only invoke Tier 2 if the post-Tier-1 residuals retain systematic structure across species. The ladder’s first three tiers can be evaluated in a few hours of CPU time on a modest cluster, and the diagnostic value of comparing Tier 0 to Tier 1 to Tier 2 LL/cell substantially exceeds the value of a single calibration run.

4.3 Connections to the FIA-as-validation-anchor literature

Recent calibration work in the LANDIS-II ecosystem has moved toward Bayesian inference and evolutionary strategy frameworks (Dietze et al. 2014; Fer et al. 2018; Alaila et al. 2025 in prep). Our approach is methodologically simpler: it relies on a single log-likelihood and a frequentist optimization. The simplicity is intentional. The FIA validation anchor is uniquely well-suited to a simple LL approach because each FIA observation is essentially a controlled measurement under known protocol, with known measurement uncertainty (~10–15%), at known spatial location, and at known calendar year. The Bayesian framework’s strength — handling prior uncertainty over the parameter space and propagating it through the posterior — provides less marginal benefit when the validation anchor itself is well-characterized.

The forward-projection multi-cycle hindcast design we use also makes a different kind of validation guarantee than the standard practice of fitting to aggregate state totals. State totals can be matched by any number of parameter combinations that misfit the underlying plot-level dynamics in compensating ways. Per-plot, per-cycle validation eliminates that degeneracy: a Tier 2 fit that matches every individual plot’s trajectory also automatically matches the state total. The converse is not true. We urge state-scale LANDIS-II practitioners to consider per-plot validation against FIA cycles rather than aggregate-level comparisons.

4.4 Calibration-then-projection: why the split matters

We calibrated using only untreated FIA plots — plots with no recorded harvest or natural disturbance in any cycle. This restriction is essential to identifying the succession-only parameters cleanly. If we had calibrated against the full FIA sample, including disturbed plots, the optimizer would have absorbed disturbance-driven mortality into reduced productivity multipliers, producing parameters that under-represent succession capacity in plots that won’t experience that disturbance in projection mode.

This calibration-then-projection split is conceptually parallel to the train-test split that machine-learning practitioners use to avoid overfitting. Calibration on undisturbed plots produces succession parameters; projection scenarios that explicitly include disturbance extensions then apply those parameters in the presence of disturbance. The calibrated state of the world is thus separable from the scenario specification, which is what landscape modelers typically want for forward simulation studies.

A subtle point: the calibration is sensitive to the definition of “untreated.” Our criterion required no recorded TRTCD or DSTRBCD in any cycle. This excludes plots affected by recorded disturbances but does not exclude plots affected by sub-clinical disturbance (drought stress, infection by less destructive pathogens, near-canopy mortality of co-occurring species). Some of the per-plot residuals we attribute to calibration error may instead reflect sub-clinical disturbance signals that FIA’s protocol does not record. We accept this as a limitation and note that the residual SD floor we observe (~0.30 even at Tier 2) likely includes some such irreducible noise.

4.5 Limitations and future directions

Four limitations stand out.

First, the single-cell scenario architecture removes dispersal effects from the calibration, which is appropriate for parameter identification but means our calibrated parameters reflect a successional process without spatial seed competition. For projection studies at multi-cell scales, dispersal kernels need to be added to the model specification (LANDIS-II handles this natively); the question of whether such addition introduces new systematic biases requires its own validation, which we leave for future work.

Second, the multi-cycle hindcast validation horizon is at most 20 years (the 2001-to-2022 window). Long-horizon (50- to 100-year) biomass trajectories produced by the calibrated model are extrapolative; they assume that the productivity parameters captured in our 20-year window apply throughout the century. Climate change makes this assumption increasingly tenuous. We discuss how the upcoming PERSEUS factorial study (Section 4.6) addresses this through explicit climate scenario integration.

Third, the calibration is state-scoped. Our θ vectors for Maine, Georgia, and Washington are not directly portable to other states. The framework is portable — any state with an FIA tree-list can run the same calibration pipeline in a few thousand core-hours of compute — but the calibrated parameters themselves are not. We provide all code and per-state best-fit θ vectors as supplementary materials to encourage replication on additional states.

Fourth, our Georgia Tier 2 attempt did not yield a credible per-species calibration. The per-plot LANDIS sub-pipeline succeeded for only a fraction of the 779-plot Georgia target subset (mean n = 10.3 paired observations per candidate, maximum n = 82, zero candidates with n ≥ 100). Production Georgia calibration in this release is therefore Tier 1 uniform θ = 0.30, which has a single free parameter and is robust under the validation framework. Identifying the root cause of the Georgia plot-pipeline failure (likely a plot-ID mapping issue specific to the Georgia per-plot scenario builder) and re-running Tier 2 with the v1.0 sample-size guard MIN_N_PAIRS = 300 is the highest-priority next step for the framework. The detailed failure-mode analysis is in our open-source repository at docs/GA_T2_failure_memo.md. The cross-state result — Maine Tier 2 per-species + Washington Tier 2 per-species + Georgia Tier 1 uniform — still demonstrates the directional asymmetry finding that anchors this paper.

4.6 Implications for the PERSEUS scenario factorial

The calibration framework developed here is a prerequisite for a class of LANDIS-II applications that has been growing rapidly but remains under-validated. State-scale factorials that vary climate, harvest, and disturbance regimes are routinely used to inform forest carbon policy, biodiversity-management plans, and adaptation strategies (e.g., Cousineau et al. 2017; Janowiak et al. 2018; Liang et al. 2022). Such factorials report differences across scenarios, and an honest reading of our results suggests those differences are quantitatively defensible only if the underlying succession parameters are calibrated to plot-level data. Otherwise the scenario differences are interpreted on top of a baseline that may already be biased by 30%, and the policy-relevant magnitudes can shift substantially.

The PERSEUS project (Weiskittel et al. 2024, in prep) applies the calibrated parameter sets from this paper to a 27-cell climate × harvest × disturbance factorial across all three states. We document elsewhere how the Tier 2 calibration changes the rank ordering of scenarios for carbon-stock and harvested-product comparisons under realistic management trajectories. Suffice it to say here that the calibration ladder we describe is the first methodological step in any defensible state-scale scenario analysis built on LANDIS-II.

Section 5. Conclusions

We have developed and applied a four-tier calibration framework for the LANDIS-II Biomass Succession extension, anchored against the multi-cycle FIA inventory cycle as a per-plot, per-year validation dataset. Across Maine, Georgia, and Washington — three states spanning the major forested biomes of the conterminous United States — we find that literature-derived parameters produce systematically biased biomass predictions whose direction (over- or under-prediction) and magnitude vary substantially by state. Uniform productivity scaling captures most of the bias in Georgia and Washington (Tier 1 LL gains of +63,554 and ~+1,800 respectively); per-species adjustment via CMA-ES is required to fully resolve Maine’s bias (Tier 2 LL gain of +2,144). The calibration changes long-horizon biomass asymptotes by 7–31 percent and substantially compresses the residual variance that would otherwise propagate into derived applications.

The framework requires only public FIA data, an open-source LANDIS-II install, and modest cluster compute. We release all code, scenario-builder scripts, calibration drivers, and per-state best-fit θ vectors at https://github.com/CRSF-UMaine/perseus-multistate-calibration under MIT license, with a permanent Zenodo deposit at publication time. We hope this framework becomes a baseline calibration step for state-scale LANDIS-II applications, replacing the current widespread practice of literature parameter transfer with quantitative anchoring against the most consistent landscape-scale forest measurement dataset available.

The next paper in this series applies the calibrated parameter sets in a 27-cell climate × harvest × disturbance factorial across the same three states, using validated disturbance extensions (Original Wind, Original Fire, Climate BDA / SBW & SPB & MPB, and Hurricane v3) and the unified factorial scenario builder developed alongside this calibration work.
